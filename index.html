I am a musicologist working at the intersection of empirical performance studies, music historiography and music education. I completed my Ph.D. in Musicology at the University of Aberdeen in 2025, with a thesis titled Tempo and Performance Style in Recordings of Beethoven’s Cello Sonatas, 1930–2012.

My doctoral research is the first systematic empirical analysis of stylistic change across the complete recorded history of Beethoven’s five sonatas for piano and cello. From a corpus of more than one hundred movement-level recordings spanning eight decades, I derived bar-level tempo data at millisecond resolution using a manual measurement protocol I developed specifically for polyphonic chamber recordings, on which automated beat-detection tools systematically fail. The work has yielded a 346-page thesis, five preprints, and an open dataset and analysis code in Python and MATLAB.

Three threads run through the research: a methodological contribution (a validated manual tempo-measurement protocol and a spline-CDF smoothing procedure for density estimation); a substantive finding about stylistic change — an ecological model in which coexisting tempo traditions shift in relative density rather than a single style evolving, identified through k-means clustering; and a historiographical critique that reads Czerny’s, Moscheles’s and Kolisch’s editorial tempo indications against the evidence of the twentieth-century performing tradition.

Alongside my research I have spent over five years in senior music leadership in UK independent schools — I currently serve as Director of Music (Junior Department) at Notting Hill and Ealing High School in London — self-funding my doctorate while designing research-informed music curricula from primary through pre-university level.

I trained as a cellist before turning to research, with a Master’s (distinction) from Codarts Rotterdam and a Bachelor’s from the Liceu Conservatory of Barcelona. My current priorities are a monograph proposal, an extension of the methodology to Beethoven’s violin sonatas and string quartets, and a hybrid manual-and-automated audio-analysis pipeline for polyphonic chamber recordings.

selected publications

  1. arXiv
    Towards Revised Tempo Indications for Beethoven’s Piano and Cello Sonatas: Czerny, Moscheles, Kolisch, and Recorded Practice 1930–2012
    Ignasi Solé Piñas
    arXiv preprint. arXiv:submit/7494591 [cs.SD] , Apr 2026
    Abs Bib

    Presents the first systematic empirical assessment of Czerny’s, Moscheles’s and Kolisch’s historical metronome indications against a corpus of over one hundred movement-level recordings. Documents systematic gaps averaging 11–20% in fast movements and 37–39% in some slow movements, and shows that Kolisch’s 1943 markings align more closely with recorded practice than either Czerny’s or Moscheles’s. Proposes revised tempo indications, presented as ranges grounded in the statistical modal tempi of the recorded corpus, as evidence-based reference points for performers and scholars.

  2. arXiv
    Coexisting Tempo Traditions in Beethoven’s Piano and Cello Sonatas: A K-means Clustering Analysis of Recorded Performances, 1930–2012
    Ignasi Solé Piñas
    arXiv preprint. arXiv:submit/7492708 [cs.SD] , Apr 2026
    Abs Bib

    Applies k-means clustering (k=3, k-means++ initialisation, 100 random restarts) to bar-level BPM data from more than one hundred movement-level recordings. Reveals that every movement supports at least two and usually three discrete tempo traditions whose internal regression slopes are negligible across 1930–2012. Proposes an ecological model of stylistic change in which coexisting traditions shift in relative prevalence rather than a single tradition evolving, and finds no correlation between cluster membership and performers’ generational, national or pedagogical backgrounds.

  3. arXiv
    Evolving Performance Practices in Beethoven’s Cello Sonatas: Tempo, Portamento, and Historical Interpretation of the First Movements
    Ignasi Solé Piñas
    arXiv preprint arXiv:2502.00030, Jan 2025
    Abs arXiv Bib

    The first paper of the series. Integrates analyses of 22 historical recordings, examines Beethoven’s metronome markings as understood through Czerny and Moscheles, and documents the diminishing use of audible portamento in the latter half of the twentieth century alongside a gradual increase in tempo after 1970. Introduces the concept of “silent portamento” as a new analytical variable for studies of expression in string performance.
