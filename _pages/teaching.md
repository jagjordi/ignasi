## Teaching experience

I have taught continuously since 2017 — first as a practical instrumental teacher and chamber-music coach, then as a classroom teacher of music, and since 2022 as **Director of Music at Notting Hill and Ealing High School** in London, where I design and lead the music curriculum for more than three hundred students from Year 3 through Year 13. While my classroom teaching has been at primary and secondary level, I have consistently operated at a level that bridges school and tertiary pedagogy: I have mentored Year 12 and 13 students through Oxbridge and conservatoire applications, supervised individual research projects on performance and historical topics, and prepared bespoke teaching resources on analytical and historical writing about music.

My approach has been shaped by two convictions. The first is that **each student can be drawn into engaged intellectual work** if the teacher is willing to meet them where they are and then move with them toward where they could go. The second is that **analytical thinking and creative exploration are mutually reinforcing** rather than opposed: students taught to listen carefully and ask precise questions make more adventurous performers and composers, and students with active creative practices bring an interpretative sharpness to analytical work. These convictions inform the curriculum I design, which integrates performance, composition, listening and written analysis from the earliest years.

## Teaching philosophy

I teach with a strong **preference for evidence and example**. In my research I have argued that aggregate claims about performance practice can be misleading when the underlying population is plural, and I try to apply the same scrupulousness to teaching. When I introduce a concept — rubato, historically informed performance, the classical sonata — I do it through contact with the primary material: students listen to specific recordings, look at specific scores, and make their own observations before any received framework is offered. The framework then arrives as a way of organising what they have already heard, rather than an authority to be accepted on trust. This is slower than a lecture-led style but produces a deeper and more durable understanding.

I also believe firmly in **modelling uncertainty and disagreement**. Musicology, and especially empirical performance studies, is a discipline in which reasonable scholars disagree about what the evidence shows. Teaching well means making that disagreement visible rather than hiding it behind a textbook consensus: showing students how a scholar builds an argument, where the weak points are, and how an alternative view would be constructed. This prepares them for the independent critical work that university study requires.

## What I am prepared to teach

Given my research background and teaching experience, I am prepared to design and deliver undergraduate and postgraduate modules on, among others:

- Empirical methods in performance studies
- History of cello performance and chamber music
- Historically informed performance; Beethoven's chamber music
- Introductory quantitative methods for musicologists (tempo analysis, basic statistics, data visualisation)
- Score and audio analysis
- Academic writing for music students

I would also co-teach modules on music technology and recording history, and contribute to supervision of undergraduate and Master's dissertations in performance studies and recording analysis. At postgraduate level, my methodology is well suited to supporting doctoral students whose projects require large-corpus recording analysis, empirical testing of historical performance claims, or **hybrid manual-and-automated audio analysis** — an interdisciplinary space bridging musicology and music-information retrieval in which my manual protocol provides a useful starting point.

I am candid that my classroom teaching has not been at university level, and I recognise that university teaching makes different demands. My five years of senior music leadership have given me substantial experience of curriculum design, teaching-team management, and sustained communication with students, colleagues and parents; my doctoral work, produced in parallel with full-time leadership, demonstrates the research capacity and self-discipline that university roles require. I would welcome a structured induction into university-level teaching, and I view that development as a natural continuation of a trajectory that has taken me from performance to teaching to research.
